REFLECTION-1

Reading this paper changed how I think about AI statistics — including the industry statistics I collected for my own research in this assignment.
What surprised me most was the historical argument. I had always thought of statistics and AI as separate things: statistics is the old way of analyzing data, AI is the new way. Friedrich et al. show that this is simply wrong. Random forests and support vector machines — methods I associate with machine learning — were developed by statisticians. The history of AI cannot be cleanly separated from the history of statistics. This made me realize that a lot of the "AI vs. statistics" framing in public discussions is more of a branding issue than a real intellectual divide.
The causality section was the most practically useful part of the paper for me. When I was reading industry reports for Topic 5, I kept seeing claims like "developers using AI tools are 55% faster" or "AI adoption leads to higher productivity." I treated these as facts about what AI does. After reading Friedrich et al., I see them differently. These are associational findings from observational surveys. The developers who adopt AI fastest might also be the most skilled, the best resourced, or working in the most productive environments. We cannot know from a survey alone whether AI caused the productivity improvement or whether something else did.
This matters practically. If a company reads "AI tools increase productivity by 55%" and implements a mandatory AI tool adoption policy, they might be disappointed with the results — because the 55% figure was not a controlled experiment, it was a correlation in survey data. Friedrich et al. give the language to explain why: confounding.
The three failure examples were eye-opening. Google Flu Trends seemed like a perfect AI success story — using search data to track flu outbreaks in real time, without waiting for hospital reports. It failed because search behavior is not illness. Microsoft's chatbot Tay was shut down in 16 hours because its training population (whoever chose to interact with it on Twitter) was not representative of any real human community. The Apple Heart Study had 400,000 participants but was poorly designed for its actual research question.
All three failures had the same root cause: insufficient statistical thinking at the design stage. The technology worked. The statistics were wrong. Reading these examples made me more skeptical of large-scale AI statistics in general — not because the tools are bad, but because the data collection and study design behind them often has hidden flaws.
What concerns me about AI in software engineering after reading this paper is specifically the lack of uncertainty communication. Industry reports state findings as clean percentages: "84% of developers use AI tools." But what is the margin of error? What is the confidence level? Which countries and platforms were included? What assumptions were made? This information is almost never in the headline. Friedrich et al. argue this is not just a communication problem — it is a scientific problem. A result without uncertainty quantification is an incomplete result.
As a future software engineer, I will be reading, producing, and acting on statistics about AI tools. This paper taught me that asking "how was this measured?" is not being overly critical — it is basic scientific literacy. I think this kind of critical thinking about data is exactly the human skill that becomes more valuable as AI generates more statistics, not less.
How my thinking changed: Before this paper, I thought statistics and AI were in competition — that machine learning was replacing traditional statistical methods. After reading it, I think they are in partnership, and that the engineers who understand both will make better decisions than those who only understand one. The ability to evaluate whether an AI claim is supported by proper study design, representative data, and appropriate uncertainty quantification — that is not a purely technical skill. It is a thinking skill. And it is one I want to develop.

REFLECTION-2

What struck me most about this paper is that it was written by some of the most senior statisticians in the world — and they are genuinely worried. Not about AI replacing humans in general, but about statistics specifically losing its voice in how AI is developed and evaluated.
Panel I's concern about "dwindling talent" resonated with me. If the best students are choosing AI and machine learning programs over statistics, who will ensure that AI systems are built with proper rigor? The authors' answer — that statisticians need to reposition themselves as essential partners in AI, not competitors — seems right to me.
The education point from Panel III is the one I find most personally relevant. The push to modernize curricula while keeping foundational principles like uncertainty and reproducibility at the center describes exactly the kind of engineer I want to be: someone who can use modern AI tools but also knows when to question their outputs.
Overall, this paper confirmed something I was already starting to think: the most valuable skill in the AI era is not knowing how to use the tools — it is knowing how to evaluate them critically.

REFLECTION-3

"Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity"
This paper was the most surprising source I read for this assignment. The finding that AI slowed experienced developers down by 19% — when they expected to be 24% faster — is the kind of result that forces you to reconsider assumptions.
What I found most interesting is not the slowdown itself, but the prediction gap. Developers, economists, and ML experts all predicted significant speed improvements. All of them were wrong in the same direction. This tells me that the belief that AI boosts productivity is so strong that even experts cannot predict accurately when it does not. It raises a question I had not considered before: how much of the productivity statistics we read in industry reports is shaped by expectation rather than measurement?
The paper also clarified something important for me about methodology. Most statistics I collected for Topic 5 — Stack Overflow surveys, JetBrains reports, GitHub data — are observational. Developers self-report how much faster they feel. Becker et al. actually measured task completion time. The gap between what developers felt and what the data showed was significant. That gap is worth remembering every time I read a headline productivity figure.
For my own future as a software engineer, this paper is a reminder that critical evaluation of tools matters as much as adopting them.
